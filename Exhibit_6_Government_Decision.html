Exhibit 6 · Decision framework

Toll money must go to capital projects, and each project should pass an evidence check first

How the money can be used, why wider roads are not the answer, and what to check before spending.

1. Where toll money can go (NY Public Authorities Law §553-j)

80%NYC Transit and MTA Bus: subway, bus and station projects
10%Long Island Rail Road
10%Metro-North Railroad

Not allowed: everyday running costs, such as driver wages.

2. Why not just widen roads?

Research on US cities (Duranton & Turner, 2011) finds that new road space fills up with new driving, so traffic returns to where it was.

Pricing manages demand in the core. To keep people out of their cars, transit from the outer boroughs has to be a real door-to-door alternative.

3. Candidate corridors, in suggested order

Corridor What to build Measure first
Eastern Queens (Fresh Meadows) Redesigned feeder route; covered transfer bays at Kew Gardens Walking routes to stops; how evenly Q30 buses arrive
East Bronx (Throggs Neck) Bus lanes and signal priority on Crosby Ave, feeding the 6 train Bx8 speed and on-time rate; how many local drivers go to the zone
Southeast Queens (Rosedale) Transfer link at Nostrand Ave; one capped LIRR + subway fare on OMNY Daily LIRR-to-subway transfers; revenue effect of a single fare

4. Three checks before spending

  1. Measure first. Collect bus speed and on-time data on each route before committing money.
  2. Confirm the destination. Check how many local drivers actually go to the zone (south of 60th St), not just to Upper Manhattan.
  3. Fix the main barrier. Make sure the project targets the biggest delay: walking, waiting or the bus ride itself.

What this shows

  • Toll revenue can only pay for long-lasting capital projects in the MTA programme.
  • Each corridor has a specific build option and a specific thing to measure before building it.
  • Money should go where a delay has been measured, not where one is assumed.

Read with care

  • Final eligibility depends on MTA capital programme rules and approvals.
  • The corridors listed are candidates, not approved projects.

Sources: New York Public Authorities Law §553-j; Duranton & Turner (2011), “The Fundamental Law of Road Congestion”, American Economic Review.
